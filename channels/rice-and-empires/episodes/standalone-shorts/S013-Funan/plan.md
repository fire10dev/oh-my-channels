## Story direction (owner 2026-11-23)
v1 told the story in time order. The owner said it was **"like a school textbook"**. v2 is told as a **paradox and a detective story**.
- **Opening paradox:** a Roman emperor's gold was dug out of the Mekong mud.
- **Answer, in the middle of the video:** no Roman brought it. Traders passed it from port to port across the Indian Ocean. Funan was a crossroads of that trade.
- **Closing paradox, a callback to the opening:** the gold travelled halfway around the world and reached us. The kingdom's own name did not.

Rules for telling it:
- **Say "Funan" within the first 5 seconds.** On the first draft the owner asked "La Mã là sao?" ("why Rome?"). Rome is the clue. The video is about Funan.
- Mix statements and questions: about one question every two beats. If every beat ends on a question, the video sounds flat (Codex draft).
- Sound contrast: court chimes when quoting Chinese records, mud and oar sounds for archaeology.
- Visual motif: archaeology sketches "stamped" over the Chinese scroll whenever the narration checks a written claim against the ground (agy).
- Stay objective:
  - The Chinese envoys are outsiders, so their account is one perspective.
  - Treat Funan as **heritage shared by Vietnam and Cambodia**. Never say it "was really" one country's.
  - No modern politics.
- Banned:
  - "Romans came to Vietnam"
  - "lost advanced civilisation"
  - "two men who never left the port" (invented by agy)
  - "thousands of canals"
  - "China invented this kingdom"
  - "existed only on paper"

## T7 result (checked 2026-11-23 20:35 +07) — hook kept, wording changed
Primary source: B. Borell, "The Power of Images: Coin Portraits of Roman Emperors on Jewellery Pendants in Early Southeast Asia", *Zeitschrift für Archäologie Außereuropäischer Kulturen* 6 (2014): 7–43.
PDF: https://angkordatabase.asia/libs/docs/publications/the-power-of-images-coin-portraits-of-roman-emperors-on-jewellery-pendants-in-early-southeast-asia/The_Power_of_Images_Coin_Portraits_of_Ro.pdf

Borell cites Malleret 1951/1962, Coedès 1947 and Lévy 1946. Her findings:

| Finding | Borell 2014 | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| A gold disc found at Óc Eo in 1944. 19 mm across (the size of a Roman aureus), 22-carat gold sheet 0.3 mm thick, 1.4 g | cat. no. 1, pp. 10–11, 34 | A |
| Its design **copies** a coin type of Antoninus Pius issued in 152 CE | p. 11; she says Malleret already noticed it | A |
| It is **not a Roman coin**: "erroneously identified as a Roman coin" in some publications. It is an embossed thin gold sheet with a broken loop, probably worn as a **pendant** | p. 10–11 | A |
| The copy has mistakes: a mirror-inverted N, garbled letters in the legend, and the beard drawn as a row of dots | p. 11 | A (describe as "the lettering is garbled") |
| The piece is **lost**. It was held in the Musée Blanchard de la Brosse, Saigon (inv. MBB 3460). In 2006 the Museum of Vietnamese History, HCMC said it "could not been located after 1975" | cat. p. 34 | B: say "its whereabouts have been unknown for decades" or attribute the 1975 date to the museum |
| Where it was made is **open**: imported from southern India, or a local Southeast Asian product (Óc Eo had goldworking workshops) | pp. 28–29; local manufacture is a "strong alternative" | B: say "made in India or in the delta itself — scholars aren't sure" |
| Roman gold reached India in quantity. Of 274 Roman gold coins found in Kerala, 138 are of Antoninus Pius, and 3 are of the same 152 CE issue | p. 27 | A/B |
| The second famous Óc Eo disc, often called "Marcus Aurelius", is in Borell's view copied from a coin of **Commodus** (191–192 CE). It is held in the Museum of Vietnamese History, HCMC (inv. 2182) | pp. 12–13 | B → **do not say "Marcus Aurelius"** |

Second source still needed for the "imitation, not a coin" point, from an author other than Borell: Malleret 1962 (Borell quotes him), or another independent study, e.g. Bellina/Glover. Miko Flohr's blog (2020) only repeats Borell, so it does not count as independent.

What this means for the script:
- 🔴 Cut the old hook line "This is a Roman emperor's gold". It is wrong: the gold was not Roman.
- The paradox is **stronger** with the facts. A Roman emperor's face, in gold, comes out of the Mekong mud in 1944. Three things make it strange:
  1. It isn't Roman. Someone copied a Roman coin to make jewellery.
  2. Nobody knows for sure who made the copy: India or Funan.
  3. Today nobody knows where it is.
- The "answer" beat changes. It used to say "traders carried the gold". It now says "Roman gold flowed to India; there, or in Funan, a goldsmith copied the emperor's face to wear as a pendant". **The image travelled further than the coin.**
- The callback still holds: "The emperor's face travelled all the way to the Mekong. The kingdom's own name never reached us."

## Perspectives (≥ 2)
- **Chinese records:** Kang Tai and Zhu Ying, envoys of the Wu state in the 3rd century. Their original reports are lost. We know them only from excerpts in later histories (*Liang shu*, *Jin shu*, *Nan Qi shu*; check which).
- **Archaeology:**
  - L. Malleret's excavation of Óc Eo (1944)
  - Later Vietnamese excavations
  - Angkor Borei in Cambodia (Stark et al.)
- **Modern historians:**
  - M. Vickery, "Funan reviewed" (1998/2003)
  - G. Coedès (the classic view, now partly revised)
  - M. Stark
  - Britannica, "Funan"
  - Indian Ocean trade: research a source, e.g. Borell on Roman objects in Southeast Asia
